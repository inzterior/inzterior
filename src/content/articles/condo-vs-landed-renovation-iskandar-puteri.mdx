Iskandar Puteri is one of the few areas in Johor where we regularly quote both ends of the property spectrum in the same month — a high-floor condo unit one week, a double-storey terrace in Horizon Hills the next. On paper, the design conversation is often similar: open up the kitchen, redo the wet areas, build in some carpentry, refresh the finishes. In practice, the two projects run on almost entirely different tracks once work actually starts.

Homeowners who've only ever renovated one type of property are usually surprised by how much the *process* changes when they move to the other. This isn't about design taste — it's about logistics and structural realities that exist above and beyond whatever you and your designer agree on. I want to walk through where those differences actually show up, so you know what to expect before you're mid-project and caught off guard.

## Service lift booking and logistics

The second thing that catches condo owners off guard is logistics. Getting materials, furniture, and demolition debris in and out of a high-rise unit usually means booking a service lift or loading bay, and that booking window is controlled by the building, not by your contractor. Larger deliveries — cabinetry, flooring pallets, appliances — often need to be scheduled well in advance, and if a delivery misses its slot, it can mean waiting for the next available booking rather than simply trying again the next day.

This is a genuinely different kind of coordination problem than a landed property faces, where a truck can usually just pull up outside the house. It's also one of the reasons a good contractor working in condos will build buffer time into the schedule around deliveries — because a missed lift booking has a way of rippling into every task that depended on those materials arriving on time.

## Landed property: more structural freedom, different constraints

Landed properties in areas like Horizon Hills generally give you more room to move. Without shared walls or a building management layer to answer to, you typically have more flexibility to reconfigure layouts, and there's no service lift booking standing between your materials and your front door.

That doesn't mean landed renovations are unregulated, though. Anything involving genuine structural changes — extensions, altering the roof structure, work that affects the building's footprint — is still subject to local council approval, and that process exists whether or not the house is inside a gated community. What shifts is the day-to-day texture of the project: you're now thinking about outdoor space, contractor parking, and truck access to the site, rather than lift bookings and a management office's house rules. Different constraints, not fewer constraints.

## What this means for your timeline and budget

Put together, the biggest practical difference between the two is where the lead time sits. A condo renovation typically carries building-level administrative steps that a landed property doesn't, and those need to be built into your overall project timeline from day one — not treated as a formality that happens somewhere in the background. We go into general renovation phasing and how long each stage tends to take in our [renovation timeline guide](/articles/renovation-timeline-malaysia), which is worth a read regardless of which property type you're working with.

Cost-wise, the difference is less about one type being inherently more expensive and more about where the money and time go. Condo projects often carry a bit more coordination overhead — lift bookings, management liaison, stricter work windows that can stretch out a schedule. Landed projects can carry more cost if the scope extends to structural or exterior work that a condo renovation would never touch in the first place.
