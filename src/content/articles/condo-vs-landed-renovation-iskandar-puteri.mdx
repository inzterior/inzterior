Iskandar Puteri is one of the few areas in Johor where we regularly quote both ends of the property spectrum in the same month — a high-floor condo unit one week, a double-storey terrace in Horizon Hills the next. On paper, the design conversation is often similar: open up the kitchen, redo the wet areas, build in some carpentry, refresh the finishes. In practice, the two projects run on almost entirely different tracks once work actually starts.

Homeowners who've only ever renovated one type of property are usually surprised by how much the *process* changes when they move to the other. This isn't about design taste — it's about approvals, logistics, and rules that exist above and beyond whatever you and your designer agree on. I want to walk through where those differences actually show up, so you know what to expect before you're mid-project and caught off guard.

## Condo renovations: JMB/MC approval and house rules

If you're renovating a condo unit in Iskandar Puteri, the first gate you'll run into isn't your contractor's schedule — it's the building's Joint Management Body or Management Corporation. Most condo developments require some form of renovation plan submission and sign-off before work can begin, and that process sits entirely outside your contractor's control. It's a building-level administrative step, and every development runs it a little differently.

Beyond the initial approval, most developments also carry house rules that shape what you can and can't do to a unit. Expect some form of restriction on drilling and hacking hours, limits on what you're allowed to do to load-bearing or shared structural walls, and requirements around waterproofing in wet areas like bathrooms and kitchens before any tiling goes down — since a leak in a high-rise unit doesn't just affect you, it affects the neighbour below. None of this is unusual, but it does mean a condo renovation has a compliance layer that a landed property simply doesn't.

The practical takeaway: don't assume you can start work the moment your contractor is ready. Check with your building's management office early — ideally before you finalise a design — so the approval process is running in parallel with design work rather than sitting as a delay after everything else is already decided.

## Service lift booking and logistics

The second thing that catches condo owners off guard is logistics. Getting materials, furniture, and demolition debris in and out of a high-rise unit usually means booking a service lift or loading bay, and that booking window is controlled by the building, not by your contractor. Larger deliveries — cabinetry, flooring pallets, appliances — often need to be scheduled well in advance, and if a delivery misses its slot, it can mean waiting for the next available booking rather than simply trying again the next day.

This is a genuinely different kind of coordination problem than a landed property faces, where a truck can usually just pull up outside the house. It's also one of the reasons a good contractor working in condos will build buffer time into the schedule around deliveries — because a missed lift booking has a way of rippling into every task that depended on those materials arriving on time.

## Landed property: more structural freedom, different constraints

Landed properties in areas like Horizon Hills generally give you more room to move. Without shared walls or a building management layer to answer to, you typically have more flexibility to reconfigure layouts, and there's no service lift booking standing between your materials and your front door.

That doesn't mean landed renovations are unregulated, though. Anything involving genuine structural changes — extensions, altering the roof structure, work that affects the building's footprint — is still subject to local council approval, and that process exists whether or not the house is inside a gated community. What shifts is the day-to-day texture of the project: you're now thinking about outdoor space, contractor parking, and truck access to the site, rather than lift bookings and a management office's house rules. Different constraints, not fewer constraints.

## Noise and work-hour rules

Both property types are subject to some form of restriction on when noisy work can happen — condos are generally the stricter of the two, with permitted drilling and hacking windows set by building management on top of whatever the local council allows. Landed properties tend to have more flexibility day-to-day, but they're not exempt from local by-laws governing construction noise; a house in a residential neighbourhood is still subject to the same municipal rules that apply to any other construction activity in the area.

What this means in practice is that neither property type gives you a blank check on scheduling. Before your contractor starts anything genuinely loud — hacking tiles, drilling into concrete, structural demolition — it's worth confirming what hours are actually permitted for your specific building or neighbourhood, rather than assuming your friend's experience in a different development applies to yours.

## What this means for your timeline and budget

Put together, the biggest practical difference between the two is where the lead time sits. A condo renovation typically needs its JMB/MC approval sorted before hacking or major work can start, which means that approval step needs to be built into your overall project timeline from day one — not treated as a formality that happens somewhere in the background. Cost-wise, the difference is less about one type being inherently more expensive and more about where the money and time go. Condo projects often carry a bit more coordination overhead — lift bookings, management liaison, stricter work windows that can stretch out a schedule. Landed projects can carry more cost if the scope extends to structural or exterior work that a condo renovation would never touch in the first place.
