Most living room inspiration you'll find online is shot in houses with windows on every wall and nothing built up against either side. That's not what a terrace or link house actually is. In Johor Bahru specifically, where terrace and link houses make up a huge share of what people are actually renovating, the living room usually shares a wall with a neighbour on one or both sides, gets light from the front and possibly a rear air well only, and often has the staircase and front porch crowding directly into its footprint. Generic living room advice doesn't account for any of that. Here's what actually matters when the layout you're working with is a terrace house, not a show-home render.

## Start with what the house's shape already gives you

A terrace or link house living room is usually long and narrow rather than square, because the whole structure is built long and narrow to fit the row. That shape isn't a flaw to disguise — it's the starting condition to design around. Fighting it with furniture arranged for a square room usually just makes the space feel more cramped than it needs to. Instead, we look at how the room's natural shape can define zones without adding walls: a seating area closer to the front where light enters, and a secondary zone — reading corner, TV console, whatever the household actually needs — further back, using furniture placement and area rugs rather than construction to mark the boundary.

## Light comes from fewer directions than you'd expect

With party walls shared on one or both sides, most terrace houses only get direct natural light from the front façade and, if the layout includes one, a rear air well or courtyard. That's a real constraint, not something a paint colour fixes on its own. What actually helps: keeping sightlines from the front door through to the back of the house as unobstructed as possible, so whatever light does enter travels further into the room instead of stopping at the first piece of furniture; reflective-but-not-glossy finishes on larger surfaces; and layered lighting that doesn't rely on one central fixture, since a single ceiling light in a long, narrow room tends to leave the far end dim regardless of how bright the fixture itself is.

## The staircase and the porch are part of the living room, not separate from it

In a lot of double-storey terrace and link houses, the staircase lands directly in or right beside the living room, and the front porch or car porch sits immediately adjacent to it — sometimes only nominally separated by a grille or half-wall. Both of these get treated as afterthoughts in a lot of renovations, and both are actually core to how the room functions. A staircase that isn't accounted for in the furniture layout ends up as a walking obstacle in the middle of the seating arrangement. A porch that's left as pure transition space, rather than intentionally decided as either fully outdoor or semi-enclosed extra room, tends to become the household's dumping ground for shoes, bikes, and whatever doesn't have another home — which then visually spills into the living room the moment the front door opens.

## Storage placed where the house actually needs it

Because the entrance opens more or less directly into the living space in most terrace layouts, a dedicated shoe and outdoor-gear storage unit right at the entry does more for how the room feels day-to-day than almost anything else — it intercepts clutter before it reaches the seating area. Beyond that, the same principle applies here as in any small-footprint space: built-in storage that uses vertical wall space, sized to actually contain what the household owns, rather than open shelving that just gives clutter somewhere new to be visible. Working with an existing terrace or link house shell — rather than a blank floor plate — usually means more attention to what's structural (party walls, load-bearing elements) versus what can genuinely be reconfigured, which is part of why we walk the actual space during a [discovery consultation](/articles/interior-design-consultation-johor-bahru) rather than quoting from a floor plan alone. ## Closing

A terrace or link house living room isn't a smaller version of a detached-house living room — it's a genuinely different shape with its own light, storage, and circulation problems to solve. Designing around those constraints, rather than importing inspiration shot in a completely different kind of house, is usually the difference between a living room that looks good in photos and one that actually works every day.
