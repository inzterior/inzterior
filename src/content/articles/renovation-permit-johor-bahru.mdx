Most homeowners planning a renovation in Johor Bahru assume there's one local authority to deal with, if they think about permits at all. In practice, "Johor Bahru" covers four separate city and municipal councils, and knowing which one has jurisdiction over your specific address is step one — because renovation approvals aren't handled by a single, centralised JB office. Get this wrong early and it adds weeks to a project timeline before any work actually starts, which is worth knowing before assuming the paperwork can be skipped or handled later.

## "Johor Bahru" isn't one council

Johor Bahru City Council (MBJB) governs the city centre and areas like Larkin, Tebrau, Ulu Tiram, Bandar Dato Onn, Kempas, Tampoi, Johor Jaya, and Permas Jaya. Kulai Municipal Council (MP Kulai) covers Kulai, Senai, Saleng and Indahpura to the north — the one most people forget. Johor Bahru Tengah Municipal Council (MPJBT) is the name you will still hear, but it stopped existing on 22 November 2017, when it was upgraded and renamed Majlis Bandaraya Iskandar Puteri; MBIP's portal still files the old MPJBT forms under an /mpjbt/ path, which is why the name survives. Iskandar Puteri City Council (MBIP) administers Iskandar Puteri itself — Skudai, Gelang Patah, Taman Perling, Kangkar Pulai, Tanjung Kupang, Tanjung Pelepas, and the surrounding townships, including Horizon Hills, where our own studio is based. Pasir Gudang City Council (MBPG) covers Pasir Gudang, Masai, and Seri Alam to the east. The address on your title determines which one you're applying to, and each council runs its own submission process — so a permit timeline someone describes from a Tebrau renovation doesn't necessarily map onto one in Iskandar Puteri.

## What actually needs council approval

Structural work — extensions, additional floors, altering the roofline, converting a covered car porch into enclosed living space, or any change to the building's footprint — needs approval before work starts, under Section 70 of the Street, Drainage and Building Act 1974, the law all four councils operate under. Purely interior work — new kitchen cabinetry, non-structural partitions inside the existing footprint, re-tiling, rewiring, built-in wardrobes — generally doesn't trigger the same building-plan process. But the line isn't always obvious in older terrace and link houses, where a wall assumed to be non-structural sometimes isn't, so it's worth confirming with the relevant council or a qualified contractor rather than assuming, particularly if the renovation touches anything load-bearing.

## How the approval process actually runs

Applications route through each council's eBuilding system or the national OSC 3.0 Plus platform, and most split submissions into tiers depending on scope: a minor works permit for smaller alterations, a standardised terrace-house addition submission (pelan setara) for common extension types, and full building plans — requiring an appointed engineer or architect — for larger structural work. The detail that catches people out: work isn't supposed to start once the application is submitted, but once the plan is actually approved and a start-work notice (Borang B) is lodged with the council.

## What skipping it actually costs

Building without approval isn't just a paperwork risk. Under the same Act, councils can issue compounds, order work stopped mid-renovation, and in serious cases require unauthorised structures to be demolished at the owner's expense — none of which is negotiable once it's flagged, regardless of how far along the renovation is. Renovation work is visible from the street, which makes it easier for a council to notice than the purely interior changes that don't require this process in the first place.
