Bathrooms are the smallest rooms we renovate and, by a wide margin, the ones most likely to cause a problem after handover. Not because the finishes are hard to choose — most homeowners have a tile and a vanity style in mind before they ever call us — but because the parts that actually determine whether a bathroom holds up are invisible once the room is finished: what's under the floor, what's behind the tile at the wall-floor junction, and whether the exhaust fan was ever sized for the room it's in. We've been called in often enough to fix bathrooms that looked finished and functioned badly that we now walk clients through these decisions before any tile gets chosen, not after.

## Waterproofing is a system, not a coat of paint

The most common assumption we run into is that waterproofing means one thing: a membrane painted onto the floor before tiling. That's part of it, but it's not the part that fails most often. The junction where the floor meets the wall — and where the wall meets a pipe penetration for the shower mixer or floor drain — is where waterproofing membranes actually break down over time, because that's where the substrate moves slightly with temperature and moisture and a thin membrane coat can crack at the seam. A proper wet-area waterproofing job carries the membrane up the wall at least to shower height (higher for the shower enclosure itself), reinforces every corner and penetration with waterproofing tape or fillet, and gets tested — flood-tested, ideally, holding water on the floor for at least 24 hours before tiling begins — rather than assumed to be fine because it looked even when it was rolled on. In a landed house this is a maintenance headache if it fails. In a condo or apartment, a failed bathroom waterproofing job means water tracking into the ceiling of the unit below, which turns a bathroom problem into a neighbour dispute.

## Floor falls that actually drain

A bathroom floor needs a gradient toward the floor trap, and it needs to be consistent — not just present somewhere near the drain. We still see floors where the screed was laid close to flat, so water pools in low spots the tiler didn't account for, or where the fall was set correctly toward the drain but there's a high point at the shower threshold that traps water on the wrong side of it. This is entirely a screed-and-setting-out problem, decided before tiling starts, and it's very difficult to correct after the fact without breaking tiles back out. It's one of the details we check during a site walk rather than trusting a plan alone, in the same way we walk kitchens directly during a [discovery consultation](/articles/interior-design-consultation-johor-bahru) instead of quoting wet-area work sight unseen.

## Ventilation sized for the room, not the budget

Many Johor Bahru terrace houses have at least one bathroom with no external wall — a common layout constraint that puts the entire moisture load on a single exhaust fan. The fan that comes standard in a renovation package is often sized for a much smaller volume of air than the room actually holds, or vented into the roof void instead of properly ducted outside, which just relocates the moisture problem instead of removing it. This matters more in Johor's climate than it would somewhere drier — we go into the broader mechanics of why sealed, humid rooms are where materials fail fastest in our [guide to renovating for Johor's climate](/articles/johor-climate-renovation-humidity-heat-materials). For a bathroom specifically, that means matching fan capacity to room volume, ducting it to open air rather than a void, and, where the layout allows, keeping or adding a small transom or louvre for passive airflow rather than relying on mechanical extraction alone.

## Layout decisions that outlast the tile choice

Wet-and-dry separation — a shower screen, a step, or a half-wall keeping the shower area distinct from the rest of the bathroom — does more to keep a bathroom usable long-term than any finish choice. Fully open, screen-less layouts photograph well but soak the entire floor on every use, which shortens the life of everything else in the room: door frames, cabinetry toe-kicks, grout lines. Door swing and clearance matter too, especially in smaller Johor Bahru condo bathrooms, where a door swinging into a limited floor area can make a renovated bathroom feel smaller than the one it replaced. These are layout decisions, not styling ones, and they're worth settling before choosing a single tile.
