Almost every terrace and link house we work on in Johor Bahru already has some version of a wet and dry kitchen — it's how these houses are built, not a feature you have to ask for. The dry kitchen sits closer to the dining area, usually the one that shows up in photos: cabinetry, a hob for light cooking, maybe an island if the footprint allows. The wet kitchen is further back, often opening onto a rear yard or air well, and does the heavy lifting — wok cooking, boiling, anything that produces real heat, oil smoke, or steam. The renovation question isn't whether to have both. It's whether each one is actually detailed for what happens in it, because a lot of the kitchen problems we get called in to fix started with someone treating the two spaces as one continuous zone with one continuous material palette.

## Two kitchens, two different jobs

The dry kitchen is closer to a Western-style kitchen — it needs to look finished, since it's visible from the dining and living areas, and it handles lighter use: reheating, prep, a kettle, maybe a rice cooker. The wet kitchen is a working kitchen in the older sense — high heat, direct flame, oil splatter, and steam that a dry-kitchen finish was never meant to survive. Treating them as one space with matching cabinetry and countertops usually means the dry kitchen's finishes get specified into the wet kitchen too, where they don't hold up, or the wet kitchen's utilitarian finishes bleed into the dry kitchen, where they undersell a space that's meant to be presentable. Deciding what each zone is actually for, before choosing a single finish, is the first design decision — not the last one.

## Where wet-kitchen renovations usually go wrong

The most common mistake we see is enclosing a previously open-air wet kitchen without upgrading the ventilation to match. Older terrace houses often built the wet kitchen semi-outdoor by design — a grille wall, an air well, natural cross-ventilation carrying steam and smoke straight out. When a renovation encloses that space for a cleaner look or more usable floor area, and the exhaust system isn't upsized to compensate, the result is a kitchen that traps heat, oil residue, and moisture against every surface in the room. We cover the broader climate mechanics behind this — why sealed, unventilated spaces are exactly where mould and material failure show up fastest — in our [guide to renovating for Johor's climate](/articles/johor-climate-renovation-humidity-heat-materials). In a wet kitchen specifically, that means a proper exhaust hood sized to the burner output, not a decorative one, and make-up air so the extraction actually has somewhere to pull fresh air from rather than working against a sealed room.

## Materials by zone, not by kitchen

For the wet kitchen, we specify for heat and oil first, appearance second: stainless steel or solid-surface worktops that shrug off direct pot contact, full-height tiling rather than a partial backsplash, since oil splatter travels further than most people expect, and cabinetry in moisture- and heat-resistant boards rather than the same finish used in the dry kitchen. Solid timber and delicate laminates that look right in a dry kitchen tend to swell, discolour, or delaminate in a wet kitchen within a couple of years. For the dry kitchen, there's more room to prioritise how it looks and how it integrates with the dining space, since it isn't absorbing the same daily heat and moisture load — but it's still worth using the same moisture-resistant board specification we'd use anywhere else in a humid climate, since a dry kitchen sitting next to an open wet kitchen still picks up ambient humidity even if it never sees direct steam.

## The opening between them

How the two kitchens connect matters as much as how each one is finished individually. A wide, fully open pass-through looks appealing in a mood board but lets wet-kitchen heat, smoke, and cooking smells travel straight into the dry kitchen and dining area — which defeats the point of separating the two in the first place. A solid wall with no opening at all makes the wet kitchen feel cut off and awkward to service from the dry kitchen side. What tends to work best in the terrace and link house layouts common here is a partial opening — a serving hatch, a half-height wall, or a door that can be closed during heavy cooking and left open otherwise — so the household controls the boundary rather than the boundary being fixed one way permanently.

## What this means for planning and cost

Because the wet and dry kitchen genuinely need different scopes of work — different tiling extent, different exhaust capacity, sometimes different electrical and plumbing runs — it's one of the areas where a floor plan alone tends to undersell what a renovation actually involves, which is part of why we walk the space directly during a [discovery consultation](/articles/interior-design-consultation-johor-bahru) rather than quoting kitchen work sight unseen. ## Closing

A wet and dry kitchen aren't two halves of one design decision — they're two rooms with different jobs, and treating them that way from the start is what keeps a kitchen renovation from becoming a maintenance problem a year or two after handover.
