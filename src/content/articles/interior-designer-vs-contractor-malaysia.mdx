"Can't I just hire a contractor directly and skip the designer fee?" It's one of the most common questions we hear, usually from someone who's already gotten a couple of contractor quotes and is wondering why they'd pay extra for design services on top. It's a completely fair question, and I don't think the answer is "always hire a designer" — that would be self-serving and, more importantly, untrue for a meaningful number of projects.

The honest answer depends on what you're starting with: how clear your design direction already is, how many trades your project involves, and how much time you have to manage the process yourself. I want to walk through what each route actually covers, so you can make that call with real information instead of guessing.

## What a contractor-only route covers (and doesn't)

A contractor, hired directly, is fundamentally an execution partner. Give them a clear brief — knock down this wall, tile this bathroom, install this kitchen layout — and a good contractor will build exactly that, on budget and to a reasonable standard of workmanship. That's their core competency, and for a lot of jobs, it's all you actually need.

What contractor-only doesn't typically cover is the thinking that has to happen before "build exactly that" makes sense. Space planning — how a layout should actually work for how you live, not just how it currently exists. Material selection across dozens of decisions — flooring grade, cabinet finishes, tile patterns, lighting placement — each carrying real cost and aesthetic implications you're expected to weigh yourself. And if your project touches multiple trades — electrical, plumbing, carpentry, tiling — you're usually the one coordinating the sequence between them, unless your contractor happens to offer that as part of their service.

None of this is a knock on contractors. It's simply outside the scope of what "contractor" means as a role. If you walk in with a half-formed idea and expect a contractor to also make your design decisions for you, that's usually where contractor-only projects go sideways — not because the workmanship was bad, but because nobody was actually driving the design.

## What a full-service interior design studio adds

A full-service studio is built around the layer that contractor-only skips. At Inzterior, that breaks down roughly the way our team describes it on our [team page](/team): design — space planning, mood boards, and material selection tailored to how you actually live, not a template pulled off a shelf; project management — one point of contact coordinating contractors, timelines, and milestone payments so nothing falls through the cracks; and site execution — a vetted network of contractors and tradespeople carrying out the work, with progress documented at every stage.

The single point of contact piece is worth dwelling on, because it's often the part people underestimate until it's missing. On a multi-trade renovation, someone has to own the sequencing — electrical rough-in before carpentry goes in, waterproofing cured before tiling starts, painting timed so it isn't damaged by later trades. Get that sequence wrong and you end up paying for rework, not just extra time. A full-service studio absorbs that coordination as part of the service, rather than leaving it for you to manage between trades who may never speak to each other directly. ## When contractor-only makes sense

I want to be straightforward about this, because it genuinely does make sense in some situations, and pretending otherwise wouldn't be honest. If your scope is small and contained — repainting a room, replacing a bathroom's fittings, swapping out flooring in a single space — you likely don't need a full design process to get a good result. If you already have a clear, specific design direction in mind — you know the layout, the materials, the finishes, and you're confident about how they'll work together — a contractor can often execute that faithfully without needing a designer to originate it for you. And if your budget is genuinely tight, paying for design services on a small job can eat into a budget better spent on materials or execution quality.

The common thread across all three is clarity and scope. The smaller and more defined the job, and the more of the thinking you've already done yourself, the less a full-service studio adds relative to its cost.

## When a design studio is worth it

On the other end, a full renovation — especially one touching multiple rooms, multiple trades, or the overall layout of a home — is where the coordination and design-direction value compounds. The more moving parts a project has, the more expensive a coordination mistake becomes, and the more a single accountable point of contact is worth.

It's also worth it when your design direction genuinely isn't clear yet. If you know you want the space to feel different but can't articulate exactly how, that's a design problem before it's a construction problem, and it's not something a contractor is set up to solve for you. And if you simply don't have the time or inclination to manage several contractors and trades directly — chasing schedules, reviewing material samples, resolving disputes between trades — that management burden is real, and it's precisely what a full-service studio is built to absorb on your behalf.

## A simple decision framework

If you're still weighing the two, a few honest questions tend to surface the answer quickly:

- Do you already know exactly what you want — layout, materials, and finishes — or are you still figuring that out?
- Does your project involve one trade, or several that need to be sequenced and coordinated?
- Do you have the time to manage multiple contractors directly, chasing schedules and resolving handoffs yourself?
- Is this a small, contained fix, or a full renovation touching most of the space?
- If something goes wrong mid-project, do you want to be the one resolving it, or do you want a single point of contact handling that for you?

If most of your answers point toward "I know what I want, it's one trade, I have the time, it's a small job," contractor-only is a reasonable, cost-effective route. If most point the other way, a full-service studio is likely to save you more in avoided mistakes and management time than it costs in fees.

## Closing

Neither route is universally right — it genuinely depends on your scope, your clarity of direction, and how much of the coordination you want to take on yourself. What matters is making that choice deliberately, rather than defaulting to whichever option you happened to get a quote from first.
