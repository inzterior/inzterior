"How long will this take?" is one of the first questions almost every homeowner asks us, usually before we've even talked about budget or design direction. It's a fair question — and it deserves a fair answer, which is harder to give than a lot of online guides make it look.

Search around and you'll find plenty of neat little timelines: a few weeks for design, a month or two for the rest, done. In our experience those numbers are usually best-case scenarios dressed up as typical ones. A renovation timeline isn't one number — it's a sequence of stages that build on each other, and the real duration depends on your scope, your material choices, and how quickly decisions get made along the way. I want to walk through what each stage actually involves, roughly structured around how we stage payments on a project, so you have a realistic picture before planning around a move-in date.

## The design phase

Design comes first, and it covers more ground than people expect: understanding how you actually live in the space, working through layout options, selecting materials and finishes, and producing the drawings and visualizations that everything downstream gets built from. This is usually the stage homeowners most want to rush — but it's also the stage where rushing costs you the most later, because every material order and every piece of custom carpentry gets specified from what's decided here.

The pace of this stage is genuinely a two-way thing. The faster you can review options and give clear feedback, the faster it moves — a design phase that stalls because decisions keep getting deferred will always run longer than one where the client is engaged and responsive throughout.

## Materials and carpentry lead time

Once the design is signed off, materials get ordered and carpentry production begins — and this is typically the longest single stage of the whole project, by a meaningful margin. It's also the stage that's least visible to a homeowner, because most of it happens in a workshop or a supplier's warehouse rather than on your site, which can make it feel like nothing is happening even though it's often the busiest part of the timeline behind the scenes.

A few things drive how long this takes. Custom carpentry — built-in wardrobes, kitchen cabinetry, TV consoles fabricated to fit your exact layout — takes real production time that doesn't compress just because you'd like it to. Imported materials add another layer: anything sourced from overseas is subject to shipping schedules and customs clearance that are simply out of anyone's direct control, ours included. Locally available, readily stocked materials move faster than anything ordered in, produced to spec, or shipped from elsewhere. This is one of the reasons we try to lock in material selections as early and as firmly as possible during design — every week a decision is left open is a week added to this stage once you finally do decide. Material choices like these are also a major driver of overall project cost, which we break down in our [renovation cost guide](/articles/interior-design-cost-johor-bahru-iskandar-puteri).

## On-site installation

Installation is the stage most people picture when they think "renovation" — the physical work happening inside your unit or house. What's less obvious is how much sequencing matters here. Electrical and plumbing rough-in generally needs to happen before carpentry gets installed, wet-area waterproofing needs time to cure before tiling goes down, and painting typically comes near the end so it isn't damaged by later trades moving through the space. Get that sequence wrong and you end up with rework — carpentry installed before wiring is finalised, for instance — which costs you more time than doing it in the right order the first time.

How long this stage runs depends heavily on scope. A single-room refresh with minimal structural change moves faster than a full-home renovation involving layout changes, extensive wet-area work, and wall-to-wall custom carpentry. It's genuinely project-specific, which is part of why we're cautious about quoting a single number that applies to every job.

## Handover and snagging

Once installation is substantially complete, we walk through the finished space together, room by room, and put together a defect list — the small things that need adjusting or fixing before we consider the project done. Cabinet doors that need realigning, a paint touch-up, a fitting that isn't sitting quite right. This is a normal, expected part of finishing a renovation, not a sign that something went wrong.

Genuinely resolving a snag list takes some further time after the main installation wraps, and that's worth building into your expectations rather than treating handover day itself as the finish line. ## What actually causes delays

Being honest about timelines also means being honest about what pushes them out, because it happens on real projects and pretending otherwise doesn't serve anyone.

Material backorders are one of the most common culprits — a specific tile, a particular hardware finish, or an imported fixture that turns out to be out of stock or delayed at the source. Late design decisions and mid-project change requests are another: swapping a material choice or reworking a layout after carpentry production has already started doesn't just add the time for the change itself, it can mean redoing work that was already in progress. For condo projects specifically, building management approval can be a real bottleneck — the JMB or MC sign-off process sits outside your contractor's control entirely, and it needs to be sorted before hacking or major work can start. We go into this in more detail in our [condo vs. landed renovation guide](/articles/condo-vs-landed-renovation-iskandar-puteri), which is worth reading if your project is in a condo. Contractor scheduling conflicts — a trade that's booked across multiple sites and can't get to yours exactly when planned — round out the usual list.

None of these are unique to us or to any one contractor; they're the realistic friction points of any renovation in this market. What separates a well-run project from a frustrating one usually isn't the total absence of delays — it's whether you're told about them early and honestly, or left to find out by watching nothing happen on-site for a week with no explanation.
